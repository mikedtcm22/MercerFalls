# Ruth Bielak

Up: [[Hollowed Townspeople]]

**71, widowed, on Delaney two blocks off the mill road. Hollowed in autumn 1994 — eleven days — because she went into the mill looking for her husband's lunch pail. She is the only previously-hollowed person inside the [[The Siren|Siren]]'s radius who has never once walked, and she isn't protected by anything clever. She's protected by a broken tape player.**

## The anomaly

Put [[Ren Sołtys|Ren]]'s historical roster on [[Dominique Ferraro|Dominique]]'s map and every previously-hollowed person inside the circle is walking. **Except one dot, well inside the innermost ring, dark.**

**She should have been in the first three.** Closer to the mill than nearly anyone on the list, notched for two years. It reads as **a hole in a circle** — legible to any player who looks, requiring no statistics and no misdirection.

## Walt

**[[Walt Bielak]] died in [[The 1991 Mill Incident|the 1991 incident]]** — a real industrial death, in the accident that covered the hollowings. Thirty-one years on the job.

Because the place shut that same week, **his effects were never returned.** Everything he had at work is still there.

She sleeps in the front room with a portable cassette player on the windowsill, and it plays a tape of him — reading the sports page, talking on some long car trip. Something ordinary. **Five years, every night.**

**The machine is nearly dead. The belt is stretched. It runs slow.**

> *"He sounds slower than he was lately. I keep meaning to take it in."*

## Why it works — occupancy, not blocking

**Her [[Nullprints|notch]] is already full.** Five years of a stretched belt running a voice at the wrong speed, every night, sitting in exactly the frequency the Siren calls on. **There is no room in her for anything else to get in.**

**Content is irrelevant. Only speed matters, because speed is the frequency.** [[Bash Whitlock|Bash]]'s deduction, and he should get to make it out loud. Converts directly into the Talkboy — see [[Episode 3 — Second Shift]].

**If she had gotten the machine fixed, she would be walking to the mill.**

## Why this cannot come from the party

> [!danger] Dominique-only
> Four teenagers do not get told about a dead husband's voice on a cassette. It's in her bedroom, at night, and **Ruth doesn't know it's relevant.** It comes out sideways, in the kind of conversation an eighteen-year-old has with a lonely seventy-one-year-old over an hour and a cup of coffee.
>
> **The party goes door to door and gets names. [[Dominique Ferraro|Dominique]] goes to one house and stays.** Per the Adult Access ladder, PCs asking a widow about her sleep is a **DC +5** situation — let them try and fail before Dominique goes.

## Her own hollowing — autumn 1994

**She went in looking for the lunch pail.**

Three years after Walt died she finally went through the boxes, and the one thing that wasn't there was **the old dented pail he carried for thirty-one years.** It isn't in the house. She's certain. So somewhere in that fog she decided it must still be at the mill.

**A sixty-nine-year-old widow walked up the mill road in September, went through a fence gap she'd known about since she was a girl, and went looking for a building she had never been inside but had heard described at her own kitchen table for three decades.** She knows the layout of that mill better than most men who worked there, because she listened to it her whole married life.

She got in. **She got nowhere near his station** — she had no idea it was that far down, because Walt described the work and never the depth. Something took her a short way past the door.

Eleven days later she was fine, and nobody ever asked where she'd been.

> [!note] She does not know that she failed
> She has no memory of the trip. **She genuinely doesn't know whether she found it.** That's why she stopped looking — not fear, just the absence of any reason to try again.

## The lunch pail — a minor quest object

> [!danger] Where it is, and why
> **[[The Worked Floors|Mill workers went down there as part of the job.]]** Walt's station was deep. **The pail is exactly where he set it down**, on a hook, the week the mill closed, and nobody has moved it in five years.
>
> Place it on **Floor 2 or below**, wherever worked infrastructure sits. It is not guarded, not magical, and not a puzzle. **It is a dented lunch pail on a hook in a room full of lockers**, and the party will walk past it unless they know to look.

### What's inside — and she doesn't know

She wanted the pail because it was *his* — the single most-handled object he owned, in his grip every day for thirty-one years. **She has no idea anything is in it.**

Under a rusted thermos and a wax-paper wrapper gone to powder, tucked flat against the inside of the lid:

- **A photograph, cut down with scissors to fit.** It's a bad photo — she's laughing with her eyes shut, it's slightly blurred, and there's a stranger's elbow in the corner. **He kept the bad one.**
- **A ticket stub.** A dance at the Armory, autumn 1947. Two admissions.
- **A paper napkin folded to the size of a stamp**, with a phone number on it in her handwriting. The exchange prefix hasn't existed since 1961.

**He carried the night they met down a shaft into that thing, every shift, for thirty-one years, and never told her.**

### Running the return

**They open it** — four teenagers in a dungeon sorting through a dead man's sandwich wrapper, finding out what he was like, then having to decide how to hand it over.

**Or they give it to her sealed** and she opens it at her kitchen table with them standing there. She doesn't know either, so the table watches her find out in real time. Slower, and the better scene if you can get it.

**Either way she is not effusive.** She goes quiet, puts the napkin down, and says something small and off-topic. That's a seventy-one-year-old receiving the largest possible thing.

### What it's worth

**Nothing mechanical, and keep it that way.** What it buys is that **Ruth starts talking** — and a woman who absorbed thirty-one years of mill-shift conversation at her own kitchen table knows an enormous amount about that building. She becomes a standing research node the party *earned*: an old-lady [[Timothy]] for the mill specifically.

> [!note] Do not say this out loud
> [[Nullprints|Release]] requires an object proving that a choice of theirs outlived them. **The party will be holding a textbook example, for entirely unrelated reasons, well before they learn what release is.** Let them handle the concept before they have a name for it.

## Open dials

- **What Walt is saying on the tape.** Unwritten, and it matters more than it looks — the party will hear it. Recommended: something completely mundane, mid-sentence, with a laugh in it somewhere.
- Whether she ever gets the machine fixed. *(If she does, and the party knows what that means, that's a whole scene.)*
- Whether telling her about the song would take the protection away.
- **Held in reserve:** making Walt one of the hollowed seven instead of a true industrial death. Devastating — she'd be playing his voice half a mile from his ghost — but it makes Ruth a far bigger figure than this role needs, and puts a release rite with her name on it. **Doesn't expire.** See [[Walt Bielak]].
